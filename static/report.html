Croton-on-Hudson is a village of roughly 8,300 people tucked into 4.69 square miles along the Hudson River. It maintains its own police department, volunteer fire department, paid EMS service, and a full Department of Public Works. With housing proposals advancing, ADU zoning in effect, and affordable housing lotteries drawing thousands of applicants, a natural question emerges: can the village’s services and finances absorb meaningful growth?

This report examines that question through the lens of the village’s actual budget line items, meeting transcripts, and comparisons to 16 peer villages in Westchester County.

I. Where the Money Goes

The village’s FY2025–2026 adopted general fund budget totals approximately $23.2 million.1 The FY2026–2027 budget, adopted in April 2026, is $24.5 million with an $8.2 million capital program.2

II. Police: The Leanest in Westchester

Croton’s police department operates with 22 sworn officers—one chief, zero lieutenants, six sergeants, one detective sergeant, one detective, and 12 patrol officers—plus a civilian dispatcher and a parking enforcement officer.1 The FY2025–2026 adopted police budget is $3.91 million.

In 2024, the village prepared a comparison of police departments across 17 Westchester villages.3 At the time, Croton had 21 officers and a $3.66 million police budget. The finding is striking:

Croton’s $3.66 million police budget (since increased to $3.91M) was the lowest of all 17 villages in the comparison—despite having the 4th largest population and the 2nd largest land area. Per-capita police spending of $441 was among the lowest, exceeded only by Hastings-on-Hudson ($373) and Dobbs Ferry ($379), both of which serve significantly different geographies.

The police union contract runs through 2029.4 The department recently upgraded to a shared Motorola Computer Aided Dispatch system with Yorktown and Briarcliff Manor police departments, and invested $414,301 in an Axon body-worn camera program.5

What 10% Growth Means for Policing

At 9,020 residents, Croton’s officer-to-population ratio would drop from 2.65 to 2.44 per 1,000—still above Sleepy Hollow (2.76 per 1,000 at 10,500 pop with 29 officers) and Rye Brook (2.73 per 1,000 at 9,900 with 27 officers). The current staffing of 22 officers is described as the highest level since 2009.5 The data suggests the department could absorb moderate growth without immediate staffing additions, though the village’s large land area (4.69 mi²) and rail corridor create coverage demands that raw population ratios don’t fully capture.

III. Fire Department: A Volunteer Force Under Pressure

Croton’s fire department is entirely volunteer. It operates five companies across three firehouses—Grand Street, Harmon (30 Wayne Street), and Washington Engine on North Riverside Avenue—with no full-time paid firefighters. The FY2025–2026 fire department budget (A3410) is $654,640.1

The largest expense items are the LOSAP retirement program ($165,027), vehicle repairs ($80,000), workers’ compensation ($65,000), physicals and cancer insurance ($43,130), and energy costs ($40,000).1 The department employs a part-time secretary ($18,813) and part-time caretaker ($12,000) but no paid operational staff.

Shrinking Roster, Rising Calls

The department’s most pressing challenge is demographic, not financial. At a Board of Trustees meeting on April 9, 2026, the Fire Chief stated plainly:

“Forty years ago when I was chief, we had a 200-man roster. We’re probably looking at 70 or 80 people at this point in time. And our population is increasing.”
— Fire Chief, Board of Trustees meeting, April 9, 20266

Simultaneously, call volume is at record levels. The Fire Chief reported at the October 29, 2025 Board meeting that the department was on track for approximately 500 calls in 2025—“another first in the community.” Top responder Joe Strini answered 281 calls that year—more than one every two days.7 Between January and October 2025, the department responded to 50 calls inside the Metro-North rail yards alone.7

False alarms account for a significant portion of call volume. At the February 25, 2026 budget work session, officials noted: “There are so many more alarm systems today than there were 25 years ago. Everybody has an alarm system… every time somebody burns their toast, it sets off the alarm system and the fire department responds.”8

Only 2–3 new adult applicants joined outside of the high school program in the past year.6 One trustee suggested investing $75,000 in a marketing and recruitment campaign to avoid the structural shift to a paid department, noting: “If we ever had to go to a paid kind of career model, we’re talking millions of dollars in increase in property taxes.”6

Significant Recent Investments

Despite the volunteer recruitment challenge, the village has made substantial capital investments in fire and EMS capacity:

  • New EMS building addition at Harmon Firehouse (30 Wayne Street): originally estimated at $2.8 million by the architect, the village DPW completed the project in-house for approximately $1.5 million, saving nearly half. EMS moved in December 2025.9
  • New Seagrave Marauder pumper: $1,789,929 to replace the 20-year-old Engine 119 at Washington Engine firehouse. Due for delivery approximately 2030. The old engine will be retained as a spare.10
  • Traffic signal preemption installed at the Harmon firehouse intersection, allowing apparatus to control the signal when leaving the station.11
  • Fire inspector upgraded to full-time in the FY2026–2027 budget.12
  • EMS youth program doubled from ~25 to 50 eligible students, enabled by the new facility space.13

Is This Normal for a Village This Size?

Yes. Nationally, 82% of all fire departments are volunteer or mostly volunteer, and 95% of volunteer firefighters serve communities under 25,000 people.14 In Westchester County specifically, every comparable village—including Ossining (pop. 27,551) with 470+ volunteer members—relies on a volunteer fire department.15 No Westchester village has transitioned to a career fire department.

The nationwide volunteer firefighter population has declined from 897,750 in 1984 to 676,900 in 2020—a 25% drop.14 Croton’s decline from 200 to ~80 members mirrors this national trend. But here, growth may actually be part of the solution rather than the problem.

How Growth Protects the Volunteer Model

The fire department’s core challenge is not call volume or equipment—it is recruiting enough volunteers. A larger population directly expands the recruitment pool. As a trustee noted at the April 2026 meeting, many neighbors “had no clue that we were volunteer here.”6 More households mean more potential members, more awareness, and a deeper bench for the junior program that already has a waiting list of ~50 young people.9

The fiscal surplus that growth generates—approximately $1.5 million per year combined—also provides resources to invest in volunteer retention and recruitment: the proposed $75,000 marketing campaign, expanded LOSAP benefits, better equipment, and the kind of facility improvements (like the $1.5 million EMS addition) that make service more attractive. A village that is fiscally healthy can afford to invest in its volunteer infrastructure; a village under fiscal pressure cannot.

In this sense, moderate growth is protective against the risk of needing to transition to a paid fire department—the single most expensive service change the village could face. A career fire department for a village of Croton’s size would cost an estimated $3–5 million per year, roughly doubling the combined fire and EMS budget.15 Every year the volunteer model remains viable is a year the village avoids that cost. Growth helps sustain it.

This hybrid model—paid EMTs stationed in the building for immediate response, supplemented by volunteer responders—achieves an average response time of approximately 8 minutes.16 The new EMS facility at Harmon Firehouse, completed in December 2025, gave the service its first permanent home after years of temporary quarters.9

A proposed EMS LOSAP (Length of Service Award Program) for 13 eligible EMS volunteers is heading to voter referendum on November 3, 2026, at an estimated cost of $35,000 per year. The fire department’s existing LOSAP, expanded in 2023 with over 80% voter approval, costs $165,027 annually.16

Combined, fire and EMS spending totals $1.49 million, or $180 per capita. While fire department costs have been described in meetings as having “nearly doubled in the past five years,”8 much of that increase reflects the build-out of paid EMS coverage and capital investments rather than operational bloat.

VI. Financial Position

Strengths

  • Tax cap compliance: The village has overridden the property tax cap only once in its history, more than a decade ago. The FY2026–2027 levy of $13,822,004 is $82,543 under the cap. Since the tax cap began in 2012, the village has saved taxpayers over $1.1 million compared to allowable levies.8
  • Conservative revenue budgeting: Revenues routinely exceed projections. In the most recent completed fiscal year, revenues came in $1.1 million above budget.4
  • Strong non-tax revenue: Parking permits generate nearly $3 million, with train station revenues running $170,000 over budget. A solar facility at the train station will produce $400,000+ annually for 30 years.8
  • Fund balance health: General fund unassigned balance is approximately $11 million, at ~25% of the adopted budget—well within the village’s target range of 17–25%.4
  • Moody’s Aa2 credit rating affirmed.17
  • NYS fiscal stress score: 3.3 with an environmental stress score of 0.0—the best possible ratings from the State Comptroller’s fiscal monitoring system. The village has never been classified under any fiscal stress category.25
  • Three consecutive surpluses: FY2024 surplus of $442,673. FY2025 revenues exceeded budget by $1.2 million (5.2%) while expenditures came in $294K under budget. Total positive variance: $1.73 million. Capital spending of $3.66 million in FY2025 included debt reduction and the Harmon Firehouse renovation.26
  • General fund balance: $13.1 million total, with $6.28 million unassigned (FY2024).26

Risks

  • OPEB liability: The village carries a $39 million Other Post-Employment Benefits liability (beginning of FY2025)—retiree health coverage for all current employees from hire date to death. State law prohibits the village from funding this in an irrevocable trust, meaning the liability grows with interest. Actual annual cost is approximately $1.3 million.4
  • Total outstanding debt: $27 million, with the FY2026–2027 capital plan adding approximately $4.8 million in new general fund debt.8
  • Health insurance escalation: Premiums increased 8.8% (family) and 8.9% (individual) in the current year. Employee benefits at $6.23 million represent 27% of the general fund—the single largest category.8
  • Salaries drive costs: At approximately 42% of the adopted budget, personnel costs are the largest structural expense. Every new position adds not just salary but decades of pension and OPEB liability.4

VIII. Water: Designed for Nearly Double the Current Population

The village’s drinking water comes from three wells drawing from a valley-fill aquifer beneath the Croton River, treated with chlorine and corrosion control additives at the well field. The system includes four storage tanks, one booster pump station, and a distribution network of pipes, valves, and hydrants serving approximately 99% of village residents.37

The most significant finding on water capacity comes from the village’s own planning documents:

The 2003 Comprehensive Plan estimated the village could support a population of 15,000 residents when fully developed. The current population of 8,327 is roughly 55% of that theoretical capacity.
— Village of Croton-on-Hudson Comprehensive Plan, 200338

At 10% growth to approximately 9,100, the village would reach just 61% of its designed capacity. The Environmental Assessment Form for the 100-unit Lot A development confirmed in July 2024 that “the existing public water supply has capacity to serve the proposal,” estimating the project’s demand at 8,178–16,940 gallons per day.39

Infrastructure Condition & Investment

The distribution system is aging but actively maintained. A 15-year cement-lining program cleans and relines older water mains. Annual leak detection surveys identify breaks before they surface. A major break on Alcott Avenue in March 2026 exposed a cast iron pipe from 1908—still in service, and repaired with village-wide service restored within 30–45 minutes.18

The FY2026–27 water fund includes $1 million in capital projects, with three active or planned water main projects:

  • Lounsbury Road — water main cleaning and relining (in design/approvals)
  • Harrison Street & Sunset Trail — water main reconstruction (estimated $1.2M, federal grant applications submitted)
  • Route 129 to DPW — water main extension, funded from PFAS lawsuit settlement payments

Water rates have been held flat for two consecutive years, and consumption has been increasing—suggesting the system is neither financially nor operationally strained at current demand levels.18

The village participates in the Indian Brook-Croton Gorge Inter-Municipal Watershed Protection Overlay District, a five-municipality coalition with 200-foot wellhead buffers, 150-foot wetland buffers, and development density limitations to protect the aquifer.37

Risk factor: The aquifer-based system is sensitive to drought. A state of emergency was declared in November 2024 due to drought conditions. Long-term climate resilience of the groundwater supply warrants monitoring.

Remaining Infrastructure Questions

  • Sewer coverage: Some areas of the village are served by private wastewater treatment plants with uncertain ownership. Some properties are forced onto septic systems. The Planning Board requires applicants to demonstrate that private plants have “sufficient capacity” for new development.19
  • Traffic: The most recent traffic study (Old Post Road, January 2026) found no speeding issues and no crashes at the studied intersections. The Planning Board recently added authority to require traffic impact studies during site plan review.21

IX. Where Growth Would Come From

Active growth vectors in Croton include:

  • ADUs: A rezoning to allow 50 accessory dwelling units was identified at the November 4, 2025 Planning Board meeting as the cause of “projected growth and anticipated future needs.”22
  • Maple Commons: An affordable housing development with a 6,000-person waiting list, subject to lottery requirements.23
  • Lot A (Croton Point Avenue): Selected developer WBP LLC, funded by Westchester County’s Housing Implementation Fund—not village tax dollars.23
  • Occupational housing preferences: The village adopted local law giving affordable housing preference to emergency service workers, medical workers, and veterans.23

At the January 14, 2026 Board of Trustees meeting, a trustee asked how the “resident income ratio” metric would be impacted “if our population growth in the future starts shifting as we build more affordable housing” with potentially less income flexibility to absorb tax increases.24

How Many New Employees Would Growth Require?

Croton’s operating structure is built to scale without proportional staffing increases:

  • Police (0–1 new officers): The department already operates at the lowest budget of 17 comparable Westchester villages, with 22 officers at its highest level since 2009. At 9,020 residents, the officer-to-population ratio drops to 2.44 per 1,000—still comparable to Rye Brook (2.73 at 9,900) and above Dobbs Ferry (2.34 at 11,500). The shared Motorola CAD system with Yorktown and Briarcliff adds dispatch efficiency without headcount.3
  • Fire (0 new hires): Entirely volunteer. Growth does not add to village payroll.6
  • EMS (0 new hires): Paid coverage operates through the OVAC contract ($740,467/year), not village employees. Scaling means renegotiating a contract, not creating permanent positions with pension and OPEB obligations.1
  • DPW (0–1 new workers): The 22-person crew serves existing roads and infrastructure. Infill development—ADUs, Maple Commons, Lot A—adds units to existing streets, not new roads to plow or water mains to maintain.
  • Administration (0–1 new staff): The fire inspector was already upgraded to full-time in FY2026–27. Permit volume increases incrementally.

Estimated new hires to maintain current service levels: 1–3 full-time employees—most likely one police officer and one DPW or building worker.

The village fiscal analysis tells only half the story. The Croton-Harmon school district operates on a separate $62.8 million budget funded primarily by property taxes levied on the same homeowners.20 New housing generates school tax revenue, but also sends new students who cost approximately $37,000 per pupil.

Using the Lot A fiscal analysis as a baseline, each new housing unit generates roughly $2,295/year in school taxes (the 100-unit Lot A project was projected to produce $229,491 in school tax revenue).27

At face value, the school math looks dire. But the marginal cost per student is far below the average, and this is where Croton’s declining enrollment becomes an asset:

  • Spare capacity exists. Enrollment has dropped 175 students (10%) since the 2013–14 peak of 1,701. Grade-level sections have been shrinking from 6 to 5 at multiple levels. PVC middle school dropped from 539 to 463 students in five years—a 14% decline. These empty seats can absorb new students at near-zero marginal cost (no new teacher, no new classroom).27
  • Marginal vs. average cost. The $37,000 per-pupil figure includes fixed costs (administration, building maintenance, debt service, utilities) that don’t increase with each new student. The district’s own fiscal analysis for Lot A used an instructional per-pupil cost of $19,400—roughly half the average—to estimate the marginal impact of new students.27
  • The step function. Costs remain low until a grade level exceeds its section capacity and a new section must be added. At that point, the district hires a new teacher (~$80,000–$130,000 salary plus benefits). A board member stated in February 2025: “It’s potentially 10 tenure-track salaries that we’re having to add to meet one section more per grade over the next ten years.”27

On the village side, volunteer fire, contract EMS, and an already-lean police force mean only 1–3 new hires to maintain current service levels. This is not a coincidence. Croton’s declining school enrollment and lean village operations have simultaneously created capacity on both sides of the taxpayer ledger. A combined surplus of $1.5–$1.7 million per year directed toward the village’s $39 million OPEB liability would meaningfully bend that curve—reducing the unfunded obligation by nearly 4% annually. Alternatively, it could fund accelerated infrastructure investment, build reserves, or stabilize tax rates as health insurance and pension costs continue to climb.

XI. How the Village Assesses New Development

Croton-on-Hudson has a multi-layered review process that applies environmental, infrastructure, and community impact scrutiny to new development. Recent examples illustrate the range of tools the village deploys:

Environmental Review

  • SEQRA (State Environmental Quality Review Act): All significant projects require Environmental Assessment Forms (EAF Parts 1–3). The Govea Park improvements triggered full SEQRA review after DPW discovered an active bald eagle’s nest during clearing work, requiring DEC consultation.31
  • LWRP coastal consistency: Projects in the waterfront zone must comply with the Local Waterfront Revitalization Program. The Half Moon Bay Bridge rehabilitation was reviewed against 20+ LWRP policies by the Waterfront Advisory Committee.32
  • Wetland activity permits: The 129 Scenic Drive ADU required Water Control Commission review, a rain garden built before construction, and proof of private wastewater plant capacity.33
  • Tree protection (Local Law 13): At 52 Mount Airy Road, the ZBA challenged the applicant’s environmental classification and required replacement trees equal to 90% of removed trees’ environmental value, calculated using I-Tree methodology with native species.34

Traffic & Infrastructure

  • Traffic impact studies: The Planning Board recently added authority to require traffic impact studies during site plan review. The Old Post Road North corridor study by AQRF collected turning movement counts, pedestrian volumes, speed data, and crash data.21
  • Stormwater coordination: The Lot A project on Croton Point Avenue required stormwater drainage plans coordinated among the village, WBP developer, Metro-North Railroad, and NYSDOT.35

Fiscal & Community Impact

  • Parkland reservation fees: New houses pay approximately $12,000 when no suitable parkland exists on-site. The Planning Board has debated whether to waive this for ADUs, with one member arguing they don’t “contribute to the growth of the village” and another insisting on the fee.22
  • School district fiscal analysis: For Lot A, a fiscal impact study estimated $229,491 in annual tax revenue against a student cost range of $19,400 to $426,800, depending on how many students the 100-unit affordable development would generate (estimates ranged from 1 to 22).27
  • Project Labor Agreements: The Lot A RFP required a PLA covering publicly funded infrastructure (water main extension, sewer line) as a condition of developer selection.35

XII. The Bottom Line

A 10% population increase—from ~8,300 to ~9,100—would add roughly 300–350 households and place Croton between Hastings-on-Hudson and Sleepy Hollow in size. Both of those villages operate the same service model Croton uses today.

Croton-on-Hudson runs the leanest police department of 17 comparable Westchester villages, relies entirely on volunteer firefighters, delivers professional EMS through a contract rather than permanent positions, and operates a school district with 175 empty seats below its 2013 enrollment peak. These are not abstract advantages. Together, they mean the village can absorb 10% population growth—roughly 350 new households and 70 new students—while adding only 1–3 new employees to maintain current service levels.

The fiscal math is clear: 350 new housing units would generate approximately $1.6 million in new village revenue and $800,000 in new school tax revenue annually. Against that, maintaining existing service levels requires roughly $570,000 in new village costs and $300,000 in marginal school costs. The result is a combined surplus of approximately $1.5 million per year, sustained for at least a decade even as health insurance and pension costs compound.

That surplus has immediate uses. Directed toward the village’s $39 million unfunded OPEB liability, $1.5 million per year would reduce the obligation by nearly 4% annually—transforming a structural risk into a manageable trend. It could also fund accelerated infrastructure investment, build reserves against future cost shocks, or stabilize tax rates.

The remaining unknowns are narrow: sewer coverage varies by neighborhood (some areas on private systems with uncertain ownership), and the long-term sustainability of the volunteer fire model depends on recruitment trends that affect every department in the state. The water system, designed for 15,000 residents, has ample capacity at 61% utilization after 10% growth.

What the data shows: Croton has built an unusually efficient operating model compared to its Westchester peers. Its water system is designed for nearly double its current population. Its school district has 175 empty seats. Its police department runs the leanest budget of 17 comparable villages. Its fire and EMS services don’t add to the village payroll. Moderate growth doesn’t strain this system—it strengthens it, spreading fixed costs across more households, filling seats that are already paid for, and generating surplus to address the structural liabilities that exist regardless of whether the village grows or not.
