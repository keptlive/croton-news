MEMO
Re: Using Renewable Diesel (RD) in Croton’s diesel-fueled trucks and oil-fired boilers
Date: 11/14/24
From: Lindsay Audin, chair, Croton Sustainability Committee
To: Committee members
[Note: information cited below came from US DOE, the Clean Fuels Alliance, National
Oilheat Research Association (NORA), Sprague Energy, California state data
resources, recent Village fuel bills, NY City and other press releases, and mileages for
diesel and EV garbage trucks.]
Executive Summary
An alternative to using electricity to secure carbon reductions from municipal diesel
trucks and oil-fired boilers has arisen. Switching from standard diesel and #2 fuel oil to
renewable diesel (RD) fuel made from organic wastes, e.g., used vegetable oils and
rendered fat from meat production, may yield comparable GHG reductions with faster
implementation at lower cost than EV trucks.
In areas where power plants primarily use fossil fuels (e.g., Westchester County), RD
may be easier and more cost-effective than converting to large electric vehicles, e.g.,
garbage trucks, and heat pumps to displace fuel oil for heating. In essence, this process
takes organic waste that would otherwise end up creating methane from landfills in the
South and instead converts it into very low carbon liquid fuels that displaces use of
fossil fuels.
Background
Renewable Diesel (RD) differs from biodiesel (BD). BD is typically made from farmed
soybean and palm oils that require use of fossil-fueled tractors, chemical fertilizers,
arable land, etc. RD is instead made from used cooking oils, rendered animal fats, and
similar organic waste products. RD is produced using a hydrogen treatment which
makes it chemically equivalent to petroleum diesel. It can be blended at any
concentration and transported using existing pipelines. While BD may be made with the
same feedstocks as RD, it’s typically blended with petroleum distillate at concentrations
of 20% or less for vehicle consumption due to its different chemistry. RD provides
BTU/gal comparable to standard diesel: 123,701 versus 138,000 (about 9% lower).
While BD has been in use in Europe and North America for decades, RD is a relatively
new liquid fuel. In the U.S., most RD is consumed in California, which offers a generous
subsidy for its use. San Francisco has been using it in its municipal vehicle fleet since
2015. In 2023, it became available in the Northeast and – as of November 2023 – is
4

fueling New York City’s 12,600 municipal diesel vehicles. It’s shipped to NY by barge
from Louisiana. Westchester County Airport now uses RD in its diesel trucks. No
Westchester municipalities are now using RD.
Unlike BD, RD is less temperature sensitive (storable to -4 F without issues) and is
easily mixed with standard diesel. RD entails no alteration to diesel vehicles and no
alteration to #2 fuel oil boilers. For the same gallons, Relative to diesel, RD’s NOx
emissions are 11.5% lower, and PM 2.5 particulates are 5-28% lower. SOx emissions
are the same as or lower than Ultra Low Sulfur Diesel (ULSD).
In July 2022, U.S. RD production capacity surpassed BD, which led to RD production
exceeding BD in 2023. So far in 2024, RD production has more than tripled from nearly
70,000 barrels per day (b/d) in 2021 to 240,000 b/d. Refineries producing RD are now
spreading across the U.S.
A local delivery to Croton on 11/7/24 by Sprague Energy would have cost only
$.1857/gal more than standard diesel, with an expected long-term contract price of up to
$.50 - $.75 above standard diesel. In this memo, a $.75/gal differential is used for
conservatism. On 11/21/24, RD will be available via the State’s contract for public
agencies, providing a firm price at that time.
Carbon Reduction With RD
For a municipality seeking to reduce its carbon footprint, i.e., contribution to climate
change, RD is an option for its diesel-fueled vehicles (e.g., garbage trucks) and its oilfired boilers.
Depending on the feedstock, processing, and fuel delivery method, RD reduces carbon
emissions relative to standard diesel by 50-80%, with ~70% being the most-cited
reduction. RD still produces carbon when burned, but its lifecycle is almost “carbon
neutral”: its released carbon was previously pulled from the atmosphere during growth
of the vegetable matter that produced the waste oil and fats. By displacing fossil fuel
that would add new carbon to the atmosphere, RD cuts overall carbon. The 30% that it
doesn’t reduce comes from its processing, handling, and transportation.
How Does This Relate To Electrification?
In recent years, the “electrify everything” movement has focused attention on electric
vehicles (EVs) and heat pumps (HPs) as the sole ways to cut carbon emissions from
large vehicles and heating systems. However, too little attention has been paid to
regional differences in power plant emissions to run those devices.
In New York, this issue has been clouded by citing average NY State power plant
emissions (in lb/kWh or MT/MWhr) instead of using EPA eGrid data that is specific to
each utility service area. Emissions in upstate NY (served mainly by hydro and nuclear
power plants) are (for 2023) .275 lb/kWh, while emissions behind Con Edison, i.e., New
York City and most of Westchester County are at .887 lb/kWh, more than triple that of
upstate NY. For Long Island, it’s worse: power production emits 1.209 lb/kWh. Behind
utilities in both areas, over 90% of power comes from burning fossil fuels.
5

Switching truck fuels to RD – and postponing pursuit of the EV garbage trucks,
L3 fast chargers, and the ~1000+ amp upgrade needed to run them – until our
local grid is much cleaner may therefore be prudent.
Cost And Carbon Reduction
For a diesel garbage truck getting 3 miles/gal, and an electric version getting 3
kWh/mile:
Cost:
- at $3.17/gallon, standard diesel costs 3.17/3 = $1.06/mile
- at $3.92/gallon [standard diesel + $.75/gal], RD costs 3.92/3 = $1.31/mile
- at $.25/kWh (average NYPA price), 3 x .25 = $.75/mile for EV
Carbon:
- at 3 mpg and diesel’s 22.4 lb of CO2 per gal , we get 22.4/3 = 7.47 lb/mile for diesel
- at RD’s 70% carbon cut, we get (1-.70) x 22.4 lb/gal/3 x 1.07 (BTU adjustment) = 2.4
lb/mile for RD
- at .887 lb/kWh for Con Ed power, we get 3 x .887 = 2.661 lb/mile of CO2e for an EV
garbage truck.
Local electricity thus produces slightly more carbon per mile than RD. Either
option (RD or EV) significantly cuts carbon relative to diesel, but RD does so
without needing to replace trucks, install fast chargers, upgrade power service,
increase distribution load, etc. RD can start immediately with no need to secure
EV truck grants/vouchers, use personnel time, etc.
Who Locally Is Using RD?
As of November 2023, New York City and (as of 9/20/24) Westchester County Airport
are now using RD in their trucks. The NY Port Authority started in May 2023. SUNY
Albany is planning to start using it in oil-fired boilers by January 2025. NYC previously
used a diesel blend including 5% BD to comply with a now-outdated local law requiring
BD in all diesel and heating fuels.
However, some climate activists continue to promote electric garbage trucks in Con Ed
territory. This may be due to a lack of knowledge/awareness of local RD supply options
and local power plant emissions, and/or loyalty to the “electrify everything” concept.
Upon being told of RD’s availability, one responded that “burning any fuel creates
emissions other than just carbon.” That view ignores the non-carbon emissions from
burning natural gas at power plants, e.g., benzene and particulates, and the processing
needed to remove H2S gas before it enters interstate pipelines.
Using RD Instead Of Air Source Heat Pumps (ASHPs) In Oil-Heated Buildings
Unlike boilers that get heat from burning fuel, ASHPs get heat from outdoor air. In the
analysis below, carbon emissions from using RD were found comparable or lower than
6

power plant emissions supplying ASHPs at the same site in our area – but operating
costs were higher.
Cost:
- at $3.17/gal, 138,000 BTU/gal, and 75% efficiency, #2 fuel oil costs ~$30.63/mmBTU
[Note that the price of standard diesel in Croton on 11/7/24 was only $2.4643/gal,
yielding $23.81/mmBTU.]
- at ASHP’s average COP of 3, we get ~10,000 BTU/kWh (i.e., 3412 BTU/kWh x 3 =
10,236); that translates to ~100 kWh/mmBTU. At $.25/kWh, ASHP heat then costs
$25.00/mmBTU
- RD at 75% efficiency, 123,710 BTU/gal and $3.92/gal = $42.25/mmBTU, ~70% more
expensive than the energy cost of ASHP. If, however, the capital cost for ASHP (at
$15,000 per ton, 3,000 hr/yr for heating, 20-year lifetime) is amortized across its
produced BTUs, we see an additional cost (with no financing and interest) of
$20.84/mmBTU, essentially erasing the extra operating cost of RD.
Carbon:
- use of #2 fuel oil (at 138,000 BTU/gal, 22.4 lb/gal, and 75% efficiency) produces 216
lb/mmBTU
- an ASHP would produce 1 mmBTU/10,000 BTU/kWh x .887 lb/kWh = 88.7 lb/mmBTU
- but RD produces 30% x 22.4 lb/gal x 1mmBTU/(75% x 123,710 BTU/gal) = 72.4
lb/mmBTU
Bottom line: both RD and ASHP produce 60-65% less carbon than #2 fuel oil. But
in our area, RD creates ~18% less carbon than ASHP, without needing to buy any
equipment, expand electric service, or increase load on the grid. [Note: at this
time, we have no information on who may be using RD as a boiler fuel, though
there is no technical reason to not do so.]
How Does This Compare For Heating Our DPW Building?
DPW consumes ~5,500 gal/yr of #2 fuel oil. Its underground tank hold 1,000 gals. Using
RD would increase cost by ~$4,125/yr more than oil, and ~$8,000/yr more than ASHP.
However, it would avoid ~21 MT of carbon/yr at only ~$200/MT/yr, a very low cost for
carbon reduction. An ASHP to provide the same heat would be quite large, likely costing
well over $100,000. Payback could take ~13 or more years, relative to the extra cost of
RD, while still producing the same or more GHG during that period. [GSHP is not an
option at the site because it is directly above our drinking water aquifer.]
Only if ASHP’s cost could be fully covered by grants and tax refunds, and cost
and personnel time for project development and management are free, would
ASHP make economic sense.
RD Availability In Our Area
For the last year, New York City has used RD to fuel its 12,600 diesel trucks. Its RD is
supplied by Approved Oil, based in Brooklyn [Keith Kerman is the City’s contact for that
7

contract in its Dept. of Admin. Svcs., a/k/a DCAS]. Approved gets its supply from the
largest RD vendor in North America: Diamond Green Diesel (DGD) with refineries in TX
and LA. DGD gets its organic waste oil and fats from Darling Ingredients, which collects
it from restaurant chains and meat-processing facilities in the South. Find a short video
on this at https://www.diamondgreendiesel.com/science-process.
Locally, RD is now supplied by Sprague Energy, a major diesel and fuel oil supplier that
is a past supplier to Croton. Sprague annually supplies large quantities of #2 fuel oil to
the Metro-North train maintenance yard. Its trucks often pass through Croton,
minimizing its delivered price of RD. Its trucks have 5 fuel compartments, allowing each
to deliver a variety of fuels. The tank at the Village’s Municipal Place depot has two
compartments, each holding 4,000 gallons of diesel or gasoline.
At the calculated $/MT/yr price for cutting carbon, until such time as our local grid
gets much cleaner, RD is better for cutting carbon than HVAC electrification for
the DPW building.
Potential impacts on Croton’s GHG emissions if RD was used for both truck fuel and
DPW heating
Croton uses ~28,000 gal/yr in its diesel trucks and ~5,500 gal/yr of #2 fuel oil in DPW
for a total potential RD usage of ~33,500 gal/yr.
33,500 gal/yr of diesel creates ~341 MT GHG/yr, ~24% of our total municipal carbon
footprint.
Switching to RD could cut that by ~70% (about 239 MT/yr, ~17% of our total footprint).
That’s more than all our carbon reductions efforts so far, combined.
At ~$.75 more per gallon, that would cost Croton ~$25,125 extra a year, yielding
$105/MT/yr.
HPs one-time average capital cost of $15,000 to avoid 1 MT/yr (recent average net MT
reduction from proposed garage ASHP project), and a 20-yr HP lifetime instead yields
~$750/MT/yr, excluding interest and inflation. That is over 7 times the extra cost of RD
during the same period.
Closing note
The above does not account for additional GHG reduction from keeping used vegetable
oils, fats, etc. out of Southern landfills where they would feed anaerobic organisms
producing methane. If instead burned in a standard incinerator, no carbon value would
come from the produced heat (though no net GHG would occur).
There are no waste-to-energy (WTE) plants in TX, LA, or near to Darling Ingredients to
secure useful energy from such waste. Landfills are the primary waste disposal option.
In Croton, burning local organic waste in the Charles Point WTE plant converts some of
its heat into electricity and industrial steam otherwise generated by burning fossil fuels.
But no local operation exists to collect, process, and convert local organic waste into
fuel instead of incinerating it in the WTE plant.

From: Len Simon, Village Trustee and Deputy Mayor
To: Sustainability Committee members
Re: Croton’s e-bike sharing program
Date: 11/8/2024
Project MOVER means e-bike share for Croton is coming! Ossining has been granted
$7.0 million from NYSERDA to establish an e-bike share program within its own
community and create satellite versions for nearby neighbors like Croton, which is
covered by the same grant. We’re working with their project managers now to select
where the bikes would be located in Croton and how the program would work here. I’ve
been very gratified by the positive reaction to e-bike sharing in our community. I’m also
grateful that our own Pedego electric bike store will be involved in the program.
Now that Ossining is underway, Croton can become a “satellite”, with the program
starting probably summer-fall 2025. Once in operation, access to the bikes (and
payment for use) will be handled by a phone app linked with a credit card. The app –
called Project MOVER” - tells you the number of bikes at each of 11 locations in
Ossining and the percent charge in each of their batteries. The app also unlocks the
bikes once your account is set up.
For detailed info, go to https://projectmoverbikeshare.com.
Here’s a link that tells you a bit about the program in Ossining:
https://www.villageofossining.org/project-mover.
Fully charged batteries for the bikes are stored in a truck that travels to the various sites
(11 in Ossining, probably fewer in Croton), replacing them in bikes having a low
percentage of charge. The driver makes his rounds for maintenance and replacement of
spent batteries, but the actual charging of fresh batteries occurs in an 8-foot tall cabinet
located somewhere along Main Street in Ossining.
The app has a great map of the bike sites. I toured around Croton with the project
managers last week and we looked at several locations, including the train station,
municipal building, parking lot by Vassallo, the library, Harmon business district,
Senasqua Park and Shoprite area, among others.
