- Solar Projects/Issues
Odd twist on $ savings from the firehouse solar rooftop: based on 6 months of NYPA
bills and interval data, the system has consistently cut peak demand to <10 kW,
shifting the firehouse to a consumption-only (no demand charge) electric rate. That’s
saving us more dollars, but getting us less municipal carbon credit. The extra kWh
enters the grid and displaces “dirty” power, but we’re giving it to Con Ed for free.
Jon reported that the present consumption-only rate does not allow net metering if a
PV system’s output is >10 kW (firehouse array is 36 kWp dc). If later this year night
operation of the heat pumps and low winter solar output push the peak over 10 kW,
the prior rate may be restored, allowing net metering. We will wait until then to see if
that occurs. We may then apply for Phase One Net Energy Metering (NEM) to credit
all solar output and carbon credit to the firehouse’s electric account.

- Funding Sources/Issues
As mentioned above, the $100 CREST grant from Sen. Harckham’s office has already
been allocated by the Village toward purchasing two EV police cars. While shifting
from gasoline to EV charging does cut out carbon footprint, it does so at a higher cost.
Either solar array would have eliminated a MT of carbon per year at a one-time cost of
~$9K. At 20,000 miles per year for a police EV, doing so costs over $12K.
The IRA’s proposed “Qualified Low Income Economic Benefit Project” option would
boost the ITC from 30% to 50% for solar and wind projects (<~1.5 MW) where at least
half the financial benefit (i.e., customer electric billing credits) went to households
earning <200% of the “poverty line”. A 50% ITC would encourage community solar
(but not municipal) projects previously deemed financially unviable.
Len found that federal poverty level to be $26,500 for a family of four (so 200% =
$53,000). Such subscribers would get a 20% (instead of 10%) discount. Len found
that ~12% (~350) of Croton households may be eligible. Example: a ~200 kW
community solar array would serve ~33 households of which ~17 would need to fit the
200% threshold, i.e., ~5% of those eligible. The rule may be finalized by early 2024.

- Other Reports
Based on data in a study summarized by Dave of forests in County municipalities, we
found that Croton’s trees sequester about 1,040 MT/yr of carbon. That’s slightly less
than the carbon emissions from municipal operations, and only ~1.3% of the
community’s carbon footprint. While no data was provided on municipally-owned
trees, an earlier DPW tree survey led to the same conclusion for our municipal carbon
footprint. Bottom line: our trees do cut our carbon emissions, but not significantly.
