[Graceful Piano Introduction in 3/4 Waltz Time]

[Verse 1 - Soprano with Gentle Chorus, Lilting and Elegant]*
Gushing from this living fountain,
Music pours a falling strain,
As the Goddess of the Mountain
Comes with all her sparkling train.
From her grotto-springs advancing,
Glittering in her feathery spray,
Woodland fays beside her dancing,
She pursues her winding way.

[Verse 2 - Growing Slightly Fuller]*
Gently o'er the ripling water,
In her coral-shallop bright,
Glides the rock-king's dove-eyed daughter,
Decked in robes of virgin white.
Nymphs and Naiads, sweetly smiling,
Urge her bark with pearly hand,
Merrily the sylph beguiling
From the nooks of fairy-land.

[Verse 3 - Flowing and Ethereal]
Swimming on the snow-curled billow,
See the river-spirits fair
Lay their cheeks, as on a pillow,
With the foam-beads in their hair.
Thus attended, hither wending,
Floats the lovely oread now,
Eden's arch of promise bending
Over her translucent brow.

[Verse 4 - Bold and Commanding, Full Chorus]*
Hail the wanderer from a far land!
Bind her flowing tresses up!
Crown her with a fadeless garland,
And with crystal brim the cup.
From her haunts of deep seclusion,
Let intemperance greet her too,
And the heat of his delusion
Sprinkle with this mountain-dew.

[Verse 5 - Triumphant, Fortissimo]*
Water leaps as if delighted,
While her conquered foes retire!
Pale Contagion flies affrighted
With the baffled demon, Fire!
Safety dwells in her dominions,
Health and Beauty with her move,
And entwine their circling pinions
In a sisterhood of love.

[Verse 6 - Reverent and Majestic]
Water shouts a glad hosanna!
Bubbles up the earth to bless!
Cheers it like the precious manna
In the barren wilderness.
Here we wondering gaze, assembled
Like the grateful Hebrew band,
When the hidden fountain trembled,
And obeyed the prophet's wand.

[Final Verse 7 - Grand and Prophetic, Full Choir Building to Climax]*
Round the aqueducts of story,
As the mists of Lethe throng,
Croton's waves in all their glory
Troop in melody along.
Ever sparkling, bright, and single,
Will this rock-ribbed stream appear,
When posterity shall mingle
Like the gathered waters here.

The Story Behind the Music

From Naples to New York: Rossini's Nymphs Become Croton's Goddess

The music for the Croton Ode was adapted by Sidney Pearson from the "Coro di Ninfe" (Chorus of Nymphs), No. 12 in Act III of Gioachino Rossini's opera Armida (1817). In the opera, spirits disguised as nymphs sing to enchant Crusader knights in an enchanted garden: "Qui tutto è calma, delizia, amor" ("Here all is calm, delight, and love").

Morris's genius was in repurposing this imagery. The opera's nymphs became the Croton water-nymph — an oread, a mountain goddess — traveling from her grotto-springs to save New York City. The lilting, dance-like quality of Rossini's chorus perfectly suited Morris's vision of water as something graceful, magical, almost supernatural.

Pearson adapted the Italian chorus for English lyrics, transposing the soprano parts for a mixed chorus of the New York Sacred Music Society. The resulting performance — Rossini's melody filling the air as Croton water surged from the Park Fountain for the first time — must have been extraordinary.

About the Poet

George Pope Morris (1802–1864) was one of America's most celebrated poets. Edgar Allan Poe called him "our best writer of songs." His most famous work, "Woodman, Spare That Tree!" (1837), set to music by Henry Russell, became one of the most quoted poems in America. Morris co-founded the New York Evening Mirror — the newspaper that published the first credited appearance of Poe's "The Raven" in 1845. He later co-founded the Home Journal with Nathaniel Parker Willis, which eventually became Town and Country magazine, still published today.

About the Source: Rossini's Armida

Gioachino Rossini's Armida premiered at the Teatro San Carlo in Naples on November 11, 1817. Based on Torquato Tasso's epic poem Gerusalemme liberata, it tells the story of the sorceress Armida who enchants the Crusader knight Rinaldo in her magical garden. The "Coro di Ninfe" — Pearson's source — occurs when spirits disguised as nymphs try to seduce Rinaldo's rescuers with visions of calm and delight. The irony of using an enchantment chorus to celebrate an engineering achievement was probably not lost on the educated New Yorkers of 1842.

The full score of Armida is available on IMSLP (International Music Score Library Project), including Breitkopf und Härtel (1823) and Steup (1824) vocal scores. A modern SSA edition of the Coro di Ninfe is published by Carus-Verlag.
