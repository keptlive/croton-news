Harmon firehouse rooftops: At a 3/13 meeting,
Village staff discussed how to integrate our
sustainability desires with the planned 3,000 SF
addition (see red outline in pic at right to the Harmon
firehouse. Work on it is rapidly moving ahead. Staff
expects it to be finished by late summer. Our part of that
work would include a ~10 kW solar array on its rooftop
costing ~$30,000, fully covered by our grants. Under
DirectPay, Croton would get back ~$9,000.
Atop the main building, we would (in 2025) install a ~34 kWp array (see blue strips
in rendering above) around new heat pumps (discussed below) where A/C units (the
orange rectangles) are now located. Rough cost would be ~$100,000 all of which
would come from our grants. DirectPay would refund ~$30,000.
As a pre-requisite, that work would require re-roofing the ~6,500 SF rooftop with a
new membrane layer. At ~$10/SF that $65,000 cost would come from Village capital
(existing roof is 24 years old). Neither our grants nor DirectPay will pay for roofing.
DPW solar parking canopy: Since we now know the site is too small for community
solar developers (who want ~400+ kW projects), we would need to bankroll this
project from grants. Croton Energy Group (CEG) has said that a ~50 kW canopy is
feasible. At $4.50/W, it would cost ~$225,000. DirectPay would reimburse ~$67,500.
Gouveia Park House rooftop: Brian and Len said the Village plans to move the
Recreation Dept. into it in 2025. Its ~2,000 SF flat could hold a ~20 kW array (enough
to run much of the building). Doing so would require removing a few trees shading the
roof. It would require re-roofing (a ~$20,000 Village capital cost). Our grants would
cover the ~$60,000 solar array, for which DirectPay would refund ~$6,000. A ~72 kW
ground-mounted array (enough to power electric usage at all of Croton’s parks) was
discussed but would require removing ~24 trees (unacceptable) and securing an
exemption from the park land alienation law.
- Heat Pumps
At the Harmon addition, we proposed a ~7-ton rooftop hybrid (electric/gas) ASHP
instead of the 82,000 Btuh gas-fired HVAC RTU in the present design. Assuming a
rough cost (equipment and labor) of ~$10,000/ ton and a capacity of 7 tons (i.e.,
84,000 Btuh), the ASHP would cost ~$70,000.
Grant funding of ~$14,000 would cover a 20% differential over what the Village would
have paid for a gas-only A/C (about $56,000). There should be no extra cost for the
electric or gas service (already in the base design). DirectPay doesn’t pay for ASHPs.

At Harmon’s main building, HPs in fiscal 2025 (6/25-5/26) would involve replacing six
4-ton 25-year old rooftop A/C units with hybrid electric/gas HPs in the same positions
(see orange rectangles in pic above). (An earlier design for locating HP condensers
around the perimeter were found to be too expensive and complex.) At ~$10,000/ton,
total cost would round out to ~$250,000. Since the units are already beyond their
useful life, Village capital would cover the base cost (~$200,000) if new A/C units were
instead installed, with our grants covering the 20% (i.e., $50,000) differential for HPs.
DirectPay doesn’t pay for ASHPs.
Washington firehouse garage HPs: Jon secured two proposals and felt confident
his design (using six 9,000 Btuh HPs) could be done for ~$50,000. As part of that
installation, to fully eliminate use of fuel oil at the site, we discussed replacing the
existing oil-fired boiler with a small (~12 kW) electric boiler to run only when the heat
pumps (existing and new) are unable to sufficiently heat on the coldest days of the
year. [Mayor Pugh subsequently OKed using CREST funding since the end result
would make the building all-electric while allowing decommissioning of its 45-year old
underground oil tank. Doing so will avoid issues with CEC grant funding.]
DPW HPs: To assess replacing the 25-year old A/Cs at DPW with ASHPs, a Village
Level II ASHRAE energy audit was done on 3/14. A variety of other energy/carbon
options will also be evaluated. Once we have the audit’s results, we can determine if
covering 20% of the extra cost of HPs could be justified and covered by future grants.
We should get results by our next meeting.
Regarding limitations on DirectPay tax credits, we verified that they are available for
commercial/institutional GSHPs (but not for ASHPs). For residential ASHPs under the
“Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit” program, up to $2,000 per year per home
is available, regardless of income. More may be available under other IRA-related
programs based on income, but their rules have yet to be finalized.
- Financial and Budgeting Issues
Budgeting grants: Our $507,500 of recently accrued CEC grants consist of $82,500
(from Community Campaigns), $175,000, and $250,000 (both from gathering CEC
points). To ensure we get all of it, NYSERDA (by 5/22) requires a basic budget for
how we would spend it. We then have 3 years to spend the money or else lose it.
Summing the above proposed projects (excluding any covered by CREST and net of
DirectPay), we will have a ~30% “cushion” to cover unexpected costs. The above
excludes the NY-SUN solar incentive (presently $1/W for rooftops and $1.20 for
canopies). That funding may no longer be available by the time we start the projects.
CEC may be cut back: Unrelated to the above, Len described a 3/22/24 proposed
NYSERDA plan to greatly scale back CEC in 2026. What’s left would involve only the
~50 munis now most active (~6% of the 850 participants). They would be moved into
DEC’s Climate Smart Community (CSC) program. NYSERDA appears to expect the
most active muni people) to help paid contractors support other munis without also
getting paid. Many munis (and Lindsay) submitted comments on the planned changes.
[HVRC subsequently said Croton’s leading CEC position should immunize us against
the proposed change.]
